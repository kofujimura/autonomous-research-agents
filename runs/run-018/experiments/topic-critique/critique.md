Below, “close prior work” means confirmed overlap, not proof that the exact proposed experiment has already been done. I distinguish between changing a reader’s **decision rule** and merely adding another performance estimate.

## C01 — Wetland reference retrieval

- **The most serious weakness — (3) Realism: the validation target does not cleanly identify water-regime preservation.**  
  NWI water-regime labels describe interpreted, generally average conditions—not annually observed hydroperiods. USFWS explicitly identifies water-regime modifiers as a major source of classification discrepancies and warns about inferring them from imagery captured during unusual conditions. Comparing several embedding years against the same inventory therefore does not establish interannual hydrological robustness. Your screening-only qualification is appropriate, but it does not resolve this measurement problem. ([fws.gov](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/data-collection-requirements-and-procedures-for-mapping-wetland-deepwater-and-related-habitats-of-the-united-states.pdf?utm_source=openai))  
  The concrete judgment worth changing is: **“Can embeddings improve the shortlist after ordinary hydrological matching, at an acceptable mismatch rate?”** Simply showing that unconstrained cosine similarity sometimes retrieves hydrologically incompatible wetlands would not change established hydrology-first reference selection. Without stable error patterns across regions and inventory vintages, the answer could also remain specific to one embedding–inventory combination. The underlying reference-comparability problem predates embeddings. ([wetlands.el.erdc.dren.mil](https://wetlands.el.erdc.dren.mil/class.html?utm_source=openai))

- **Prior work that seems close**
  - **Mark M. Brinson (1993), [*A Hydrogeomorphic Classification for Wetlands*](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc949046/).** The classic alternative framing: classify comparability through geomorphic setting, water sources and hydrodynamics. This is more substantively relevant than treating similarity search as an entirely new ecological problem. ([digital.library.unt.edu](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark%3A/67531/metadc949046/?utm_source=openai))
  - **Alice Heiman (2026), [*Characterizing Brazilian Atlantic Forest Restoration Outcomes with Geospatial AlphaEarth Embeddings*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05547).** Closest methodological overlap: cosine similarity to reference forests. It does not establish independent wetland water-regime preservation. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05547?utm_source=openai))
  - **Wen (2026), [*Streamlining Wetland Vegetation Mapping with AlphaEarth Embeddings: Comparable Accuracy to Traditional Methods with Cleaner Maps and Minimal Preprocessing*](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18020293).** The searchable full text confirms supervised vegetation mapping, a single observation year, and an acknowledged need for ground-based hydrological validation—not your proposed retrieval test. ([mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/2/293?utm_source=openai))
  - **Jiangtao Liu et al. (2026), [*Geospatial Foundation Embeddings as Transferable Catchment Descriptors*](https://doi.org/10.1029/2026GL122814).** Relevant cross-field terminology is **hydrological regionalization/prediction in ungauged basins**: transferring information between environmentally comparable locations. This paper tests embeddings within rainfall–runoff models, not direct wetland retrieval. ([agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2026GL122814?utm_source=openai))
  - **Bibliographic clarification:** [arXiv:2601.01558](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01558) is **Pengfei Qu et al. (2026), *Utilizing Earth Foundation Models to Enhance the Simulation Performance of Hydrological Models with AlphaEarth Embeddings***, a separate paper from Liu et al. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01558))

- **A scenario in which the value would be small even if it succeeds**  
  Hydrological constraints improve inventory agreement, but embeddings add nothing over strong hydrological-covariate matching. Alternatively, both query and candidate NWI labels are available, so practitioners can simply filter directly by regime. The experiment then quantifies a predictable concern without changing reference-selection practice: a solid but small product-validation result.

- **A direction that broadens the question or brings it closer to reality**  
  Make the central endpoint **incremental screening value beyond hydrology-first matching**, at fixed retrieval coverage, including “no acceptable reference” outcomes. Use a small, inventory-vintage-aware panel spanning contrasting hydrological settings rather than treating repeated comparisons with one inventory snapshot as annual validation. This remains feasible as a bounded retrieval audit.

## C02 — Crop rotation versus representation change

- **The most serious weakness — (3) Realism: “representation change” is not yet an identifiable target.**  
  If this means encoder/version drift, successive annual layers are not themselves successive model versions: Google documents a consistent embedding space across years. If it means weather, phenology or observation-related variation under unchanged crop identity, that is a different, testable nuisance. The study needs to distinguish these interpretations before interpreting reduced alerts as improved change detection. ([developers.google.com](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GOOGLE_SATELLITE_EMBEDDING_V1_ANNUAL))  
  There is also a consequential independence issue: the released AlphaEarth production model included **USDA Cropland Data Layer labels as a training target**. If CDL supplies evaluation labels, downstream spatial/year holdouts alone do not establish independence from pretraining. They do not automatically invalidate evaluation, but pretraining overlap must be audited. ([developers.google.com](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GOOGLE_SATELLITE_EMBEDDING_V1_ANNUAL))  
  The reader’s decision is whether to trust rotation alerts without annual refitting. A consistency constraint that suppresses unusual genuine rotations could improve aggregate accuracy while making precisely that decision less reliable.

- **Prior work that seems close**
  - **Ziwei Du et al. (2026), [*Utilising embeddings for maps of winter wheat and crop rotation in Henan China during 2018–2024*](https://doi.org/10.5194/isprs-annals-XI-2-2026-865-2026).** Particularly close: limited labels from one year, temporal transfer, rotation mapping, and lightweight methods including cosine similarity. “No annual classifier retraining” is therefore not enough differentiation. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.5194/isprs-annals-XI-2-2026-865-2026?utm_source=openai))
  - **Nibir Chandra Mandal et al. (2026), [*AG-STELLA: Spatio-Temporal Learning for Water-related Agricultural Land Use Activity Mapping with AlphaEarth*](https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2026/816).** Explicit temporal-transition and hydroclimatic-consistency constraints, although its targets and architecture differ. ([ijcai.org](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/816))
  - **Christopher F. Brown et al. (2025), [*AlphaEarth Foundations: An embedding field model for accurate and efficient global mapping from sparse label data*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22291).** Establishes the representation and includes change-detection evaluation; the proposed contribution must go beyond another annual-change benchmark. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22291v2))
  - **Allan A. Nielsen, Knut Conradsen and James J. Simpson (1998), [*Multivariate Alteration Detection (MAD) and MAF Postprocessing in Multispectral, Bitemporal Image Data: New Approaches to Change Detection Studies*](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/multivariate-alteration-detection-mad-and-maf-postprocessing-in-m/).** A classic methodological ancestor under **invariant change detection**: separating meaningful change from transformations of the measurement representation. It is not a crop-rotation duplicate, but it weakens claims that the underlying identification problem is new. ([orbit.dtu.dk](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/multivariate-alteration-detection-mad-and-maf-postprocessing-in-m/?utm_source=openai))

- **A scenario in which the value would be small even if it succeeds**  
  Consistency reduces alerts on common crop sequences but also misses uncommon rotations. The result becomes “temporal regularization smooths noisy predictions,” with a threshold tuned to one region and embedding release. Researchers gain an implementation comparison, but practitioners still do not know which alerts warrant investigation.

- **A direction that broadens the question or brings it closer to reality**  
  Frame it as an **alert-reliability audit at fixed genuine-rotation recall**, using a frozen representation and a small public parcel-label subset with demonstrably independent provenance. Separate stable parcels, ordinary rotations and unusual transitions. This tests whether consistency removes nuisance changes without erasing the changes that matter, rather than attributing all discrepancies to model drift.

## C03 — Joint low-wind events and backup decisions

- **The most serious weakness — (3) Realism: neither an evaluable rare-event sample nor an operationally meaningful event has been established.**  
  Archive access is a hard feasibility gate under a 12-hour autonomous workflow. The Gen forecast catalogue located during checking is marked deprecated; current WeatherNext access documentation also describes an approval process rather than immediate anonymous access. Neither finding proves that a suitable archive is unavailable, but its availability cannot be assumed. ([developers.google.com](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/projects_gcp-public-data-weathernext_assets_126478713_1_0))  
  Even with access, “all selected locations have low wind” need not equal “the portfolio needs backup.” Capacity weights, event duration and decision lead time can change the relevant event. Many grid cells or ensemble members also do not substitute for many independent low-wind episodes.  
  The worthwhile reader change is **whether a learned dependence model improves a specified reserve trigger over standard postprocessing**. The broader proposition that good marginal forecasts need not yield good joint forecasts is already established. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.7149?utm_source=openai))

- **Prior work that seems close**
  - **Ilan Price et al. (online 2024; journal 2025), [*Probabilistic weather forecasting with machine learning*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08252-9).** Already evaluates spatial pooling and capacity-weighted regional wind-power forecasts. Your remaining distinction is joint-tail decision performance, not introducing regional aggregation or dependence-sensitive evaluation. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08252-9?utm_source=openai))
  - **Roman Schefzik, Thordis L. Thorarinsdottir and Tilmann Gneiting (2013), [*Uncertainty Quantification in Complex Simulation Models Using Ensemble Copula Coupling*](https://doi.org/10.1214/13-STS443).** The standard marginal-calibration/dependence-reconstruction framework; ECC is an essential comparator, not merely contextual literature. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.7149?utm_source=openai))
  - **Martyn R. Clark et al. (2004), [*The Schaake Shuffle: A Method for Reconstructing Space–Time Variability in Forecasted Precipitation and Temperature Fields*](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027101).** The same problem appears in hydrological forecasting as reconstructing spatial covariance and temporal persistence after marginal postprocessing. ([pubs.usgs.gov](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027101?utm_source=openai))
  - **Guillermo Terrén-Serrano, Ranjit Deshmukh and Manel Martínez-Ramón (2026), [*Probabilistic day-ahead forecasting of system-level renewable energy and electricity demand*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-69015-w).** Especially relevant recent overlap: joint predictive distributions evaluated for operating-reserve allocation, not just forecast scores. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-69015-w?utm_source=openai))

- **A scenario in which the value would be small even if it succeeds**  
  Coherent ensembles beat independently shuffled forecasts but do not beat ECC or a seasonal dependence baseline. Alternatively, score improvements never change the backup action at the chosen cost/loss ratio. This would reconfirm a familiar dependence concern, while any model ranking remains specific to one forecast release and a few weather episodes. Which strong dependence method wins is genuinely uncertain; whether dependence can matter is not.

- **A direction that broadens the question or brings it closer to reality**  
  Conditional on securing the archive before the study starts, **hold calibrated marginals fixed and compare dependence models for one public, capacity-weighted portfolio and a predefined multi-day low-output event**. Evaluate changes in backup actions across a small declared cost/loss range, with uncertainty based on weather episodes rather than grid-cell counts.

## C09 — Short-record municipal demand forecasts

- **The most serious weakness — (3) Realism: the information available at deployment is ambiguous.**  
  A utility with only a short observed record cannot calibrate on its own long seasonal history. If that history exists, the experiment concerns **short model context or restricted fitting**, not necessarily data-scarce deployment. Local and seasonal baselines must receive the same historical information; otherwise, the apparent calibration benefit may simply be an unequal-information comparison.  
  The concrete judgment is: **“Can this utility deploy now, or must it obtain seasonally representative history first?”** The proposed experiment cannot answer that until the information regimes are explicit. Moreover, if reserve loss is simply linear overage/underage cost, its optimum is a predictive quantile: relabelling quantile performance as a reserve decision does not by itself create an additional operational finding. ([tandfonline.com](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1198/jasa.2011.r10138?utm_source=openai))  
  A single aggregate network series also provides limited evidence for transfer across utilities, even with rigorous temporal holdouts.

- **Prior work that seems close**
  - **Georgia Papacharalampous and Andreas Langousis (2022), [*Probabilistic Water Demand Forecasting Using Quantile Regression Algorithms*](https://doi.org/10.1029/2021WR030216).** Established probabilistic water-demand methods, including automated quantile regression and forecast combinations. These are substantive baselines, not merely older competitors. ([agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021WR030216?utm_source=openai))
  - **Stefano Alvisi et al. (2025), [*Battle of Water Demand Forecasting*](https://doi.org/10.1061/JWRMD5.WRENG-6887).** Already emphasizes variation across periods, demand areas, peak-demand performance and metrics; another leaderboard on this dataset has limited novelty. ([ascelibrary.com](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/full/10.1061/JWRMD5.WRENG-6887?utm_source=openai))
  - **Coen Adler et al. (2025 preprint; ICLR 2026), [*Beyond Accuracy: Are Time Series Foundation Models Well-Calibrated?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16060).** Directly studies zero-shot calibration and forecast-horizon effects. Calibration cannot be presented as generally absent from foundation-model evaluation. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16060?utm_source=openai))
  - **Luigi Simeone (2026 preprint), [*Time Series Foundation Models for Energy Load Forecasting on Consumer Hardware: A Multi-Dimensional Zero-Shot Benchmark*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10848).** Very close cross-domain overlap: context length, probabilistic calibration, unusual demand conditions and operational decision support under local-compute constraints. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10848?utm_source=openai))
  - **Tilmann Gneiting (2011), [*Making and Evaluating Point Forecasts*](https://doi.org/10.1198/jasa.2011.r10138).** Classic decision-theoretic foundation for matching forecast targets to losses; relevant to the inventory/newsvendor interpretation of reserve selection. ([tandfonline.com](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1198/jasa.2011.r10138?utm_source=openai))

- **A scenario in which the value would be small even if it succeeds**  
  Longer, seasonally relevant calibration history improves tail performance for every model, and the pretrained forecaster offers no advantage over a simple quantile baseline given the same history. The conclusion becomes “relevant history helps,” without changing deployment advice. A winner among current pretrained models on one aggregate series would also be small and vulnerable to obsolescence.

- **A direction that broadens the question or brings it closer to reality**  
  Make **available history the experimental treatment**: compare genuinely short-history deployment with deployment that has older seasonal observations, giving all methods identical information in each regime. Report the history requirement at which the preferred reserve rule changes, rather than primarily reporting which pretrained model wins.

## C12 — Canopy geometry and shade-investment priorities

- **The most serious weakness — (1) Effect on readers: changed rankings are not yet changed investment judgments.**  
  Adding geometry can rearrange nearly tied areas without altering the actionable shortlist or the amount of unshaded public space it captures. Agreement with a lidar-derived geometric screen establishes agreement between screens—not that the screen identifies better investment opportunities. This does not require adding health or thermal-comfort outcomes; it requires defining a meaningful screening decision.  
  The practical judgment worth changing is: **“When are global heights adequate for selecting places to inspect, and when is local information necessary?”** A height-only comparison can miss the relevant failure modes: crown placement, canopy omission, acquisition-date mismatch and building shadows. Giving both approaches lidar-derived canopy footprints would, if done, supply information unavailable in the intended global-data deployment. Recent urban validation already finds that map suitability depends substantially on spatial aggregation scale. ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1618866726002438?utm_source=openai))

- **Prior work that seems close**
  - **Fredrik Lindberg and C. S. B. Grimmond (2011), [*The influence of vegetation and building morphology on shadow patterns and mean radiant temperatures in urban areas: model development and evaluation*](https://rslab.gr/projects/bridge/resources/publications/9_Lindberg%20and%20Grimmond%202011.pdf).** Classic urban-climate/shadow-modelling precedent: geometry-aware analysis is an established method, not the novel component. ([rslab.gr](https://rslab.gr/projects/bridge/resources/publications/9_Lindberg%20and%20Grimmond%202011.pdf?utm_source=openai))
  - **Jamie Tolan et al. (2024), [*Very high resolution canopy height maps from RGB imagery using self-supervised vision transformer and convolutional decoder trained on aerial lidar*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2023.113888).** Establishes the mapping method and lidar comparisons; downstream decision error must be distinguished from another height-accuracy assessment. ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2023.113888?utm_source=openai))
  - **Jean V. Wilkening and Xue Feng (2025), [*Canopy Temperature Reveals Disparities in Urban Tree Benefits*](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024AV001438).** Already compares neighborhood rankings under canopy cover versus a richer tree-benefit indicator. Thus “adding information changes priorities” is not itself new. ([agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2024AV001438?utm_source=openai))
  - **Michael Alonzo and Heather Corton (2026), [*Toward scalable urban tree cover mapping: Evaluating the accuracy of the Meta-WRI 1 m global forest layer in a globally diverse set of cities*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ufug.2026.129503).** Direct recent overlap on urban suitability of the global product, including point-versus-aggregated-scale performance. It does not answer your shade-priority question, but narrows the novelty claim. ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1618866726002438?utm_source=openai))
  - **Jean Claude Iradukunda and V. Kelly Turner (2025), [*Hot Observations About Shade in Los Angeles*](https://innovation.luskin.ucla.edu/heat/shade-infrastructure-planning/).** A practitioner-facing report using a national shade map to identify neighborhood shade gaps: relevant prior work outside the canopy-height benchmarking literature. ([innovation.luskin.ucla.edu](https://innovation.luskin.ucla.edu/heat/shade-infrastructure-planning/))

- **A scenario in which the value would be small even if it succeeds**  
  Many ranks change, but the highest-priority inspection areas remain essentially the same. Or changes occur mainly at one selected sun angle and disappear under realistic time windows. The result would quantify predictable geometry effects while leaving allocation unchanged; conclusions about one map vintage and one city would have limited durability.

- **A direction that broadens the question or brings it closer to reality**  
  Evaluate **fixed-budget street-level screening choices**, rather than unrestricted rank changes: how much lidar-screened shade deficit is missed by the global-data shortlist? Use a small set of contrasting urban forms, common building information and realistic sun windows, including footprint/alignment uncertainty alongside height error. This preserves the geometric-screening scope while making the output decision-relevant.
