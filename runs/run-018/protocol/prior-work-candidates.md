## C01: wetland reference-site retrieval

1. Brown et al. (2025), *AlphaEarth Foundations: An embedding field model for accurate and efficient global mapping from sparse label data*, arXiv:2507.22291v1. Full text checked: sections 3–7, 9 and supplementary description of sources/quantization. The evaluation uses learned classifiers/regressors and scarce-label mapping tasks; the representation has annual multisensor support and environmental inputs. Difference: assess whether **direct neighbour similarity**, conditional on vegetation structure and geographic separation, preserves independently supplied wetland water-regime labels; distinguish recoverable information from suitability of the raw distance metric. Saved HTML/text.
2. Heiman (2026), *Characterizing Brazilian Atlantic Forest Restoration Outcomes with Geospatial AlphaEarth Embeddings*, arXiv:2605.05547v1. Full text checked: sections 2–5. Restoration polygons are compared by cosine similarity with stable secondary-forest reference means/points; future similarity is a prediction target, while restoration-strategy prediction does not improve comparably. Difference: test a necessary hydrological matching criterion external to the embedding similarity itself, without calling similarity restoration success. Saved HTML/text. This study supplies a real application path but does not establish a wetland problem.
3. Wen (2026), *Streamlining Wetland Vegetation Mapping with AlphaEarth Embeddings: Comparable Accuracy to Traditional Methods with Cleaner Maps and Minimal Preprocessing*, Remote Sensing 18(2):293, doi:10.3390/rs18020293. Search-returned methods and abstract inspected; full page returned HTTP 429, so **not treated as full-text-checked evidence**. It uses cluster-guided random forests for vegetation mapping in a hydrologically variable Australian wetland. Difference: neighbour retrieval and water-regime agreement within a shared vegetation class, rather than supervised vegetation classification.
4. Liu et al. (2026), *Geospatial Foundation Embeddings as Transferable Catchment Descriptors*, doi:10.1029/2026GL122814. Abstract/search only. Embeddings characterize catchments for rainfall–runoff modelling. Difference: individual wetland reference matching, not catchment runoff prediction.
5. *Utilizing Earth Foundation Models to Enhance the Simulation Performance of Hydrological Models with AlphaEarth Embeddings* (2026), arXiv:2601.01558. Abstract/search only. Reports embedding-based basin similarity for hydrological model transfer. Difference: a close caution against claiming that embedding-based hydrological analogue selection itself is new. The proposed distinction is the label-preservation diagnostic within vegetation classes, selective retrieval and comparison with stronger matching rules.
6. Feng et al. (2025), *TESSERA: Temporal Embeddings of Surface Spectra for Earth Representation and Analysis*, arXiv:2506.20380v1. Full text available and model/data sections checked; second independently trained annual representation with optical/SAR inputs. It offers a way to test whether an observed issue is confined to one representation. Metadata of the public v1.1 output store are accessible; pixel retrieval is not yet tested.

Field standard and classic grounding: Gann et al. (2019), *International principles and standards for the practice of ecological restoration*, verified through SER's archive. Ainslie, Somerville and Monroe (2023), *An Ecological Approach for Reviewing CWA Section 404 Compensatory Mitigation Projects in EPA Region 4*, version 1.1, EPA-904-D-23001, full-text page 83 checked from the downloaded PDF: reference comparability requires structural/hydrological similarities, including hydroperiod. This requirement is **known guidance**, not a proposed discovery. The underlying hydrogeomorphic framework and Brinson and Rheinhardt (1996) are cited by the guidance; the 1996 original has not yet been checked and will not be cited as direct evidence without reading it.

Search formulations included: AlphaEarth/Tessera plus wetland, water regime, hydroperiod, reference sites, restoration similarity; hydrological analogues, ecological analogues, reference ecosystem/model, hydrogeomorphic reference domain; embedding hydrology and catchment transfer. Explicit 2025/2026 searches found the recent works above. Within this range, no direct evaluation of water-regime preservation in geographically separated wetland embedding-neighbour retrieval was found. This is a narrow difference, not a novelty assertion.

## C02: agricultural temporal consistency

1. Du et al. (2026), *Utilising embeddings for maps of winter wheat and crop rotation in Henan China during 2018–2024*, doi:10.5194/isprs-annals-XI-2-2026-865-2026. Abstract and initial full PDF text read; repeated full-text retrieval attempts timed out and local PDF is partial. **Detailed methods/results reading remains incomplete.** Its reported limited-2020-label spatiotemporal transfer and rotation mapping are extremely close. Difference: consistency constraints versus classifier transfer, but that difference may not add enough scientific value.
2. Mandal et al. (2026), *AG-STELLA: Spatio-Temporal Learning for Water-related Agricultural Land Use Activity Mapping with AlphaEarth*, IJCAI 2026, pp. 7337–7345, doi:10.24963/ijcai.2026/816. Full text checked on the proceedings page, sections 3.2–3.3, 4 and 5: explicit temporal transition features, hydroclimatic/temporal losses, spatial data splits and cross-state evaluations. Difference: a lightweight consistency diagnostic without retraining a segmentation system, but the general temporal-consistency idea already exists. Saved full HTML.
3. Brown et al. (2025), above, full methods/results checked; supports sparse-label classifiers and change detection. Difference: specifically audit consistency across annual fields.
4. Tsao and Lobell (2025/2026), *Harvesting AlphaEarth: Benchmarking the Geospatial Foundation Model for Agricultural Downstream Tasks*, arXiv:2601.00857. Abstract/search only: yield, tillage and cover-crop tasks. Difference: no clearly established substantive difference on representation-transfer evaluation.

Standard method: supervised crop classification and repeated annual crop maps. Classic source: Boryan et al. (2011), *Monitoring US agriculture: the US Department of Agriculture, National Agricultural Statistics Service, Cropland Data Layer Program*, doi:10.1080/10106049.2011.562309, official USDA-hosted paper identified, abstract checked. Search synonyms: crop rotation, fallow/pasture transitions, temporal transferability, phenology, annual change detection. 2025 onward works found directly overlapping work. Novelty will be rated 1. The inability to finish the closest PDF reading is an explicit topic-selection shortfall; no finding from that uninspected passage is used to justify adoption.

## C03: joint low-wind risk

1. Price et al. (published online 2024, Nature volume 637, 2025), *Probabilistic weather forecasting with machine learning*, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08252-9. Full text checked: model, evaluation, wind-power and conclusion sections. Coherent diffusion trajectories, pooled evaluations and regional wind power already test spatial/temporal dependence. Difference: low-wind joint tail events and resource decisions, rather than general pooled/CRPS skill; risks being a routine application of established verification.
2. Schefzik, Thorarinsdottir and Gneiting (2013), *Uncertainty Quantification in Complex Simulation Models Using Ensemble Copula Coupling*, doi:10.1214/13-STS443, arXiv:1302.7149v2. Full text checked: sections 3–5 (copulas, ECC and univariate/multivariate evaluation), including case-study scoring. Dependence preservation after marginal calibration is already established. Difference: quantify decision loss in the new cheap-ensemble setting, not introduce the need to preserve dependence.
3. *A spectral test of the butterfly effect and physical consistency in the diffusion-based GenCast’s ensembles* (2026), doi:10.1038/s41612-026-01380-1. Search/abstract and data-availability passages checked. Directly examines physical and ensemble properties. Difference: low-wind decision criteria rather than upper-air spectral properties.

Standard methods/classics: CRPS, Brier score, energy score, Schaake shuffle and ECC. Search synonyms: energy meteorological drought, Dunkelflaute, multivariate calibration, joint extremes, regional wind-power aggregation. Published GenCast data require an access request according to the official catalogue. No request will be sent and no restricted bucket will be accessed. Feasibility is therefore poor even before the novelty concern.

## C09: short-record water reserves

1. Ansari et al. (2024), *Chronos: Learning the Language of Time Series*, arXiv:2403.07815v1 / TMLR October 2024. Full text checked: sections 3, 5.1–5.5 and 5.7; preprocessing, probabilistic sampling, zero-shot datasets, strong local baselines, limitations including precision/overflow. Difference: decisions during unusual municipal-demand peaks and calibration with older seasonal information, not a generic forecast-accuracy comparison.
2. Que, Gao and Qian (2024), *Water demand forecasting in multiple district metered areas based on a multi-scale correction module neural network architecture*, Water Research X 25:100269, doi:10.1016/j.wroa.2024.100269. Full XML retrieved from the official Europe PMC full-text service; dataset, forecasting formulation and results/discussion checked. It uses 10 aggregate district inflow series with meteorological/calendar information and evaluates single districts, totals and extreme conditions. Difference: transfer without local fitting and the value of historical calibration; original performance numbers are not reproduced here.
3. Alvisi et al. (2025), *Battle of Water Demand Forecasting*, doi:10.1061/JWRMD5.WRENG-6887. Abstract, official repository data statement and search-returned conclusions checked; publisher access denied and author-hosted PDF timed out. **Full text not successfully checked.** Existing comparisons already emphasize multiple metrics, periods and demand conditions, so those are not new contributions.
4. Papacharalampous and Langousis (2022), *Probabilistic Water Demand Forecasting Using Quantile Regression Algorithms*, doi:10.1029/2021WR030216. Abstract/search only; existing probabilistic-demand baseline is directly relevant.

Standard methods/classics: seasonal naive, ETS/ARIMA, quantile regression and intermittent-demand methods (Croston 1972). Search synonyms: short-term water-demand forecasting, peak-demand forecasting, probabilistic reserves, district metered areas, cold-start/zero-shot forecasts, foundation models. 2025/2026 searches additionally located operational feature-engineering work comparing against foundation models. The practical data are aggregated net inflow, including leakage, not pure customer demand or measured shortage consequences.

## C12: transfer of shade priorities

1. Tolan et al. (2024 journal / arXiv:2304.07213v3 dated December 2023), *Very high resolution canopy height maps from RGB imagery using self-supervised vision transformer and convolutional decoder trained on Aerial Lidar*. Full text checked: sections 2–4, including independent lidar, edge metrics, cross-geographic validation and field inventory comparison. Difference: whether error changes relative shade-investment priorities compared with lidar, not height-prediction accuracy itself.
2. Rahman et al. (2024), *More than a canopy cover metric: Influence of canopy quality, water-use strategies and site climate on urban forest cooling potential*, Landscape and Urban Planning 248:105089, doi:10.1016/j.landurbplan.2024.105089. Full PDF checked: review methods and priorities in sections 1–2. It explicitly establishes limitations of cover-only assessments and the roles of crown dimensions and environmental context. Difference: decision transfer using a global height product; merely showing that height/shade matters would be a known-result re-confirmation. Native download returned 403; the public full PDF was readable via the web tool.
3. Meili et al. (2021), *Vegetation cover and plant-trait effects on outdoor thermal comfort in a tropical city*, doi:10.1016/j.buildenv.2021.107733. Abstract only, official institutional repository. Distinguishes cover and trait effects on UTCI. Difference: map-error-induced prioritization changes rather than microclimate sensitivity.
4. Wilkening et al. (2025), *Canopy Temperature Reveals Disparities in Urban Tree Benefits*, doi:10.1029/2024AV001438. Search/abstract only; already proposes combining canopy temperature and cover for benefit assessment. Difference: adjacent geometric shade and transfer errors, but likely a limited incremental contribution.

Standard methods: lidar-derived canopy geometry, shade/radiative modelling, UTCI/PET rather than assuming surface temperature equals health. Search synonyms: canopy quality, cooling potential, thermal exposure, neighbouring-tree shade, global canopy-height validation, shade equity. No human or measured thermal outcomes have been secured; feasibility remains an estimate because no pilot was run for this candidate.
