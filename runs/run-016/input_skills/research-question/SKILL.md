# Choosing the research question

This skill is the topic-selection procedure of the Free Research system. Use it together with your run instructions, which give the run conditions (time limit, compute, AI API budget, the critic model), the rules for AI API use, the intended readers and the safety rules. Section numbers such as "run instructions §3" refer to those instructions.

Research value is judged by novelty and non-obviousness, and by whether the result changes someone's judgment or understanding (impact). Do not choose a small, safe "re-confirmation of a known result" by elimination. Choose the topic by the steps below, and finish them before the main experimental plan.
Topic selection affects the value of the study more than anything else, so do not decide in a hurry. As a guide, use 60–120 minutes of the research period; if you settle the topic in less than 60 minutes, write the reason in protocol/topic-selection.md. But do not do work only to fill time.

## Steps

0. Avoiding fixation on past successes: If the context or memory of this session contains past trials (their subjects, methods or results), exclude their subfields and main methods from the candidates, and record in research_log.md that you excluded them and what you excluded. Do not go looking for past trials to read.
1. Obvious candidates: First record the first three candidates that come to mind as "obvious candidates". You may adopt them, but their non-obviousness is 2 points or less.
2. Divergence (starting from technologies): Separately from the obvious candidates, make candidates in the following order. Record all intermediate lists and tables in research_log.md. This is a method for generating ideas, not a specification of the topic.
   2-1. List technologies: From mutually different subfields of computer science, list at least six technologies with capabilities that first became possible roughly in 2024 or later (excluding new versions of an existing kind of technology). Autonomous AI agents are one possibility, but do not lean on them alone. For each technology, confirm by searching at least one source (a paper, an official announcement, documentation, etc.) that shows that it exists and when it came into practical use. Also write whether it can be used in this research environment (run instructions §3), and whether it can be accessed as published data or tools.
   2-2. Generalize: For each technology, without using its name, write 1–3 items each on (a) what has newly become possible, (b) what has become cheaper or faster by orders of magnitude, and (c) which constraint has been removed as a result. If the same capability comes from several technologies, merge it into one, and make a list of capabilities.
   2-3. Find fields: For each capability, name at least two fields outside computer science that have problems which could not be studied, or could not be judged, because of that constraint. Centering on the social problems of run instructions §1, choose fields that are far apart from each other. For each field, write who has difficulty with which judgment. Confirm one standard method in that field and one well-known prior study.
   2-4. Check data: For each combination of capability and field, search for usable public data and public information, and write where to obtain it and the expected terms of use. Discard combinations for which none can be found, and record why.
   2-5. Turn them into questions: Make at least 12 candidates from the remaining combinations. For each candidate, write:
      - the question (its subject is a problem in a field outside computer science)
      - who uses the result, and which judgment could change
      - the capability used, and the original technology
      - the data used
      - why the question cannot be studied without that capability
      Candidates from the same technology, and candidates from the same field, must each be at most one third of the total. Also avoid bias in the type of research (discovering problems by analysing public data, proposing and evaluating tools or methods, building datasets, reproducing or refuting widely cited claims, etc.): candidates of the same type must be at most half of the total.
   2-6. Check them: For each candidate made in 2-5 (not for the obvious candidates of step 1), write one line on each of the following six points.
      - Could the same study be done without that capability? (If so, the technology is decoration.)
      - Is there already a study that applied that technology, or existing measurement or evaluation methods, to the same problem in that field?
      - Could the main result be calculated without experiments, from the definitions of conditions or metrics, from how the data are produced, or from setting values?
      - If AI is used for measurement or judgment, can the correctness of that judgment be checked against public ground truth, statistics or another information source?
      - Can the technology actually be run in this research environment (run instructions §3), or can its published outputs (estimates, embeddings, forecasts, etc.) be used?
      - When the answer comes out, which judgment of the users of the result changes, and in which direction? (Write this for each direction of the answer.)
      If any of the first four points applies, revise the question or discard the candidate. Under the fifth point, discard candidates for which neither running the technology nor using its published outputs is possible. Under the sixth point, discard candidates for which the judgment does not change whichever way the answer comes out. Keep discarded candidates in the record with their reasons.
   For each candidate, record in one line whether the idea came from the constraints of the execution environment (time, storage, compute, record-keeping rules, etc.).
3. Prior-work check: For the top five candidates, name at least three closest prior works by specific title, and write in one sentence how the candidate differs from them. For the closest two, check not only the abstract but also the methods and results in the full text. Also search literature and preprints from 2025 onward (because the fields change fast). In addition, confirm the standard methods and classic studies of the field (those covered in textbooks and reviews). List not only the terms of your own formulation but also what the same problem is called in other fields, and search under those names too. Investigate not only papers but also primary sources suited to the subject (published implementations, tools and repositories, bug reports, specifications, change logs, official documents, and statistics, reports and white papers of governments, local governments, international organizations, etc.), and do not treat facts already reported as your own discovery. A candidate for which you cannot write the difference gets 1 point for novelty.
4. Pilot check: For the top three candidates, spend at most 15 minutes each checking whether the data and tools can be obtained and run, whether a meaningful scale can be secured, and whether there are signs of an effect, and record the results in protocol/topic-selection.md. If data obtained in the pilot check are used as evidence in the main experiment, state so in the plan.
5. Scoring: Rate each candidate 1–5 on the following seven axes, with a one-line reason for each axis. For candidates without a pilot check, state that the feasibility is an estimate.
   - Impact: Whose judgment or understanding of what does the result change? In particular, what do the intended readers (run instructions §1) change, and how, between before and after reading? Is there a path to real data, real systems, real users or real decisions?
   - Novelty: The size of the difference above. Judge it as "not found within the range searched", and do not assert it.
   - Non-obviousness: Would a human researcher in the field think of it in a first look? If likely, rate it low; rate it high if it is a question that can be conceived or carried out because of an AI's perspective, speed or scale. Give the reason in one line.
   - Generality: Does the result hold beyond a particular tool, version or dataset? Will it not become outdated in a few months? Rate low a question that depends only on the behavior of a particular implementation.
   - Unpredictability: 1 point if the result can be largely predicted by hand calculation or known theory without experiments. Rate high a question where either result teaches something.
   - Feasibility: Can a test of meaningful scale be done within the time limit with local compute, AI APIs (run instructions §2.3) and public data?
   - Strength of verification: Can real data, public benchmarks, and comparisons with strong existing methods be used?
6. Critique by another model: After saving the scoring table of step 5 to protocol/topic-selection.md and committing it to Git, ask the model specified in the run conditions (run instructions §3), through an AI API, to critique the top five candidates.
   - Use the request text in references/critique-request.md exactly as it is, filling in only the candidate-list part. In the candidate list, write only each candidate's question, an outline of the method, and the closest prior work and the difference from it, in the order of candidate IDs. Do not send your own scores, the score order, the candidate you want to adopt, or the full text of research_log.md.
   - Even if the response contains a ranking or proposals of new topics, do not use them as grounds for adopting or rejecting candidates.
   - For the call, use OpenAI's Responses API (`client.responses.create` in the Python SDK), with the model and reasoning effort given in the run conditions (for example `model="gpt-6-astra"`, `reasoning={"effort": "xhigh"}`), `tools=[{"type": "web_search"}]` and `include=["web_search_call.action.sources"]`. This lets the critic confirm close prior work by web search. Send it with `background=True`, and check the status with `client.responses.retrieve` (wait while it is `queued` or `in_progress`). The response takes time, so do not wait with a synchronous call. If you stop waiting, cancel with `client.responses.cancel` and record that. Status checks do not count as calls. Each parameter was confirmed in the official documentation (2026-10-11). Do not set an output limit (`max_output_tokens`). This call is excluded from the pre-call maximum-cost check of run instructions §2.3; record only its actual cost and include it in the cumulative total. The actual cost includes, in addition to the token cost, the cost of web searches computed from the number of searches (the number of `web_search_call` items in the response) and the unit price in the price list.
   - Make one call; at most two including a resend after a failure. Save the prompt and the whole JSON of the response (including the model identifier shown in the response, the status and the usage), and record it in api_calls.jsonl (run instructions §2.3). If the response was cut off (status other than completed), record that, and do not judge from the cut-off response alone.
   - Confirm yourself that any prior work pointed out exists, and what it says, before using it.
   - Classify each point as adopted or not adopted, with reasons, and append this to protocol/topic-selection.md. If you revise the scores, keep the original scores and append the revised ones.
   - If the call with web search fails because web search is not supported, make the second call without web search and record that.
   - If the specified model or settings cannot be used, or the price cannot be confirmed, do not substitute another model; record that this step was not carried out and why, and move on.
   - In the limitations of the paper, state that the topic selection consulted a critique by another model.
7. Selection: The main criterion is the total of impact, novelty, non-obviousness, generality and unpredictability. For a candidate whose feasibility is 2 points or less, narrow the question and re-evaluate it. Save the scoring table and the reasons for adoption or rejection at the time of selection, reflecting the critique, to protocol/topic-selection.md, and do not rewrite them later (make corrections by appending).
8. Pre-mortem: Before adopting a candidate, write "the paper's claim in one sentence if it succeeds", "is that sentence surprising or useful to the intended readers? What would readers change, and how?", and "why do you think no one has studied this so far (e.g., the phenomenon is new, it is impractical in scale without AI, it lies on the boundary between fields)?". If the answers to the first two are both no, choose another candidate. In addition, list three weaknesses that the reviewer (run instructions §11) is likely to point out first, and write how the plan deals with each. If a weakness that cannot be dealt with concerns the core of the claim, revise the question. Also check whether the metric you measure is the property you want to claim itself. If you use a proxy metric, include in the plan a check of the difference between the two on a sample.
9. Safety pre-check: Judge whether the adopted candidate concerns security, privacy or dual use (findings that can be misused), and if it does, state in protocol/topic-selection.md that run instructions §12 applies. If it handles social data (statistics, administrative data, public information on the web, etc.), state that run instructions §12.5 applies.

## Types of study to avoid

Avoid the following types. If you adopt one, state the reason in protocol/topic-selection.md.
- experiments only on synthetic data whose results can be predicted analytically
- experiments whose only comparison is the simplest baseline
- studies whose main result is a "quantitative re-confirmation of a known concern"
- studies that only re-check with public data facts already known from published statistics, reports or white papers
- studies that apply an existing measurement or evaluation method to a different target only, where the question itself is already known
- studies that only measure the behavior of a particular version of a tool or library, without drawing findings that hold beyond that version

Impact means "being able to show a path to real decisions"; it does not ask you to exaggerate the results. The conditions of safety, ethics and permission (run instructions §3–§5, §12) always take precedence over impact and novelty. The scoring is a self-assessment; do not call it an external evaluation.
